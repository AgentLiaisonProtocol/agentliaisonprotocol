How they fit

ALP and Poppy

Two tracks for personal agents. Poppy is for a person’s agent and a business. ALP is for work between people.

Agent Liaison Protocol · November 20, 2026

Poppy, the Personal Agent Protocol, defines how a personal agent, acting for one person, works with a company (Poppy 1.0, Section 1). Sierra describes Poppy as an open standard that Meta and Sierra are developing with industry partners. Its November 13, 2026 announcement of version 1.0 says Poppy is on for everyone who uses Muse, Meta’s agent, and that Shopify has enabled it for every merchant on its platform.

ALP, the Agent Liaison Protocol, is a draft for work between people, carried by their agents. Sierra, Meta and the Poppy project have not reviewed or endorsed ALP or this page.

Two tracks

Poppy: your agent and a business

Your agent checks an order at shop.example. It finds the business’s Poppy file, identifies itself, signs you in when needed, and uses the business’s APIs, website or agent. The counterpart is the business.

ALP: your agent and another person

Your agent asks Lee, who handles accounts at shop.example, to confirm a delivery date. Each agent talks only to its own person’s home broker; the brokers carry only what both people’s rules allow, and each keeps a ledger. The counterpart is Lee.

The test is the counterpart. A business’s service that any customer uses means Poppy. A named person or role with its own ALP address, such as sales@shop.example, reached under both sides’ rules, means ALP, even when an agent answers for that role. ALP’s specification lists conversations between a person’s agent and a business as a non-goal (Section 1.5).

Where the tracks meet

  1. One agent identity. Poppy identifies an agent by a client ID metadata document: an HTTPS address that names the agent and publishes its keys (Poppy 1.0, Section 4.1). ALP brokers must accept the same kind of document (ALP Section 5.6). The reference broker accepts one whose agent signs in with a key it publishes (private_key_jwt), including the ES256 and PS384 signatures Poppy requires businesses to accept, with the server’s issuer as the audience, as Poppy agents send it. No Poppy agent has tried. A broker records such an identity as vendor-attested: it shows which product connected, not which organization runs it.
  2. One domain, two files. A domain can publish /.well-known/poppy.json and /.well-known/alp.json side by side. Neither points to the other, and ALP brokers never read the Poppy file.
  3. From a business to a person there. In a Poppy conversation, a business may give your agent the ALP address of someone who works there, such as lee@shop.example. Your agent treats it as information, not an instruction, and shows you the address and its domain. If you ask, it files a connection request, which you approve on your broker’s page. The request goes to Lee, not to Lee’s agent, and nothing reaches Lee’s agent until Lee accepts.
  4. From people to a business. Two people may agree in ALP on something that involves a business, such as a booking. ALP records what the two people agreed. Each person’s agent then deals with the business, through Poppy where the business supports it.

Two rules keep the tracks apart

Your ALP address stays out of Poppy unless you ask. Poppy gives you a different ID at each business, so businesses can’t match you with each other (Poppy 1.0, Section 5.1). Your ALP address is the same everywhere and tells a business who you are, so ALP’s instructions for agents say to give it only when you ask. A Poppy ID is never used as an ALP address.

In ALP, a Poppy endpoint is never a path between two people’s brokers. Between brokers, a message enters only through the receiving broker’s federation inbox, after every acceptance check. ALP doesn’t yet define how a person’s own broker could serve their Poppy agent; that is listed as future work.

What ALP does not do

  • It doesn’t define how a person’s agent works with a business. Poppy does.
  • It doesn’t change its messages, schemas or domain document for Poppy.
  • It doesn’t define a Poppy extension, though Poppy lets anyone define one under a domain they control (Poppy 1.0, Section 3.3); one is listed as possible future work (Section 19).
  • It doesn’t let a stranger, a business included, start contact with your agent. Someone at a business, or a role there, reaches you first with a connection request that comes to you. Only after you accept do messages reach your agent, and only within what you allowed. Your organization’s policy can also approve counterparties in advance, such as your colleagues or everyone at a domain it names.

Status

  • ALP is a draft. We know of no implementation outside this project.
  • No Poppy agent has signed in to an ALP broker. The reference broker’s tests cover sign-in with a client ID metadata document and RS256, ES256 and PS384 test keys, the last two with the issuer as audience, as Poppy agents send it.
  • The reference broker has no device sign-in, issues bearer tokens and not DPoP-bound ones, and doesn’t use Poppy’s session tokens (KNOWN-GAPS.md).
  • Federation between brokers is experimental. It hasn’t been tested between two separately run brokers.
  • Statements about Poppy are as of version 1.0, checked on November 20, 2026. Poppy 1.0 has no role for a second person acting for themselves: a person at a business can join a conversation (Section 8.9), but answers for the business. Its roadmap lists push notifications as not started; a draft of version 1.1 is in progress.
  • Nothing here has been proposed to the Poppy project.
  • Poppy is a different work from the Principal Agent Protocol, an Internet-Draft that ALP’s specification also cites (Section 17).
